# Costing Assumptions

This appendix shows how I calculated the costs in [proposal.md](proposal.md). Participation, wage replacement inputs, course fees and administration rates are **illustrative planning assumptions**, not official forecasts. All program estimates are nominal Australian dollars, rounded to the nearest million where stated. I use the same wages and unit costs across all four years. DEWR would need to update these estimates before launch.

## Option A: Individual skills accounts

| Input | Assumption |
|---|---|
| Training credit | A$4,000 per participant |
| Participation | 25,000 annually from July 2027 to June 2031 |
| Administration | 8% of training-credit expenditure |
| Annual cost | 25,000 × $4,000 × 1.08 = $108m |
| Four-year cost | **$432m** |

The design draws on Singapore's S$4,000 SkillsFuture Level-Up credit, but A$4,000 is an Australian policy assumption, not a currency conversion. Proposed eligibility starts at 30 rather than Singapore's 40, and this option excludes the income allowance available under Singapore's programme. These differences affect both access and effectiveness.

## Option B: Train before redundancy

The programme launches in January 2028. I assume 5,000 starts in the remaining half of 2027–28 and 10,000 in each subsequent financial year: **35,000 participants**, not 40,000.

| Input | Assumption |
|---|---|
| Lost net wages | $1,060 a week, an illustrative benchmark informed by ABS's $1,300 gross clerical median |
| Average training duration | Eight full-time-equivalent weeks, or 304 hours at 38 hours a week |
| Maximum duration | 320 hours, equivalent to approximately 8.42 weeks |
| Public wage replacement | 60% of lost net wages |
| Average payment | $1,060 × 8 × 0.60 = $5,088 |
| Administration | 12% of wage-replacement expenditure, including establishment costs |
| Total public cost | 35,000 × $5,088 × 1.12 = $199.450m, rounded to **$200m** |
| Employer course fees | $4,000 × 35,000 = **$140m**, additional to public expenditure |

The 60% replacement rate draws on Germany's Qualifizierungsgeld; its higher rate for parents is not modelled. Eligibility, retention conditions and delivery arrangements are proposed Australian adaptations. Payments would reflect actual wages and hours lost, not a flat grant. The net-wage benchmark is not a tax calculation for every participant.

If every participant used the full 320-hour allowance, the average payment at the assumed wage would be approximately $5,356, and B would cost approximately $210m including administration. The base estimate therefore depends on average training duration, not just the maximum entitlement.

## Option C: Clerical transition guarantee

| Input | Assumption |
|---|---|
| Career guidance | $800 per participant |
| Training voucher | $5,000 per participant |
| Participation | 8,000 annually from July 2027 to June 2031 |
| Administration | 8% of guidance and training expenditure |
| Annual cost | 8,000 × ($800 + $5,000) × 1.08 = $50.112m |
| Four-year cost | $200.448m, rounded to **$200m** |

Unit costs are planning assumptions. Guidance draws on the former Skills Checkpoint approach; the voucher is not an existing entitlement. JobSeeker remains subject to existing eligibility. I have not included changes in JobSeeker spending. A full budget assessment would need to estimate whether the programs change how long participants receive benefits.

The proposal rounds the public package to **$420m**. Individual displayed rows may differ slightly from totals because of rounding. I assume participants start early enough to finish training and receive payments within each financial year. DEWR would need to check this schedule and budget for payments carried into later years. Evaluation funding must provide for follow-up of late cohorts beyond June 2031.

## Why these pilot sizes and targets?

I propose annual capacity of 10,000 participants for B after its half-year launch and 8,000 for C. These are budget-constrained trial sizes, not estimates of the number needing support or statistically optimal sample sizes. They allow DEWR to test both delivery routes before committing to wider coverage. Provider capacity, eligible demand and an evaluation power calculation must be checked before launch. If demand exceeds available places, DEWR would need published allocation criteria and should consider random allocation among equally eligible applicants for evaluation.

The five-percentage-point employment target means one additional retained or re-employed participant for every 20 supported, assuming the comparison identifies the programme's effect. This makes the target interpretable, but does not establish value for money:

| Measure | B | C |
|---|---:|---:|
| Direct public cost per participant, including administration | $5,088 × 1.12 = $5,698.56 | $5,800 × 1.08 = $6,264 |
| Additional outcomes at a five-percentage-point improvement | 35,000 × 0.05 = 1,750 | 32,000 × 0.05 = 1,600 |
| Direct public cost per additional outcome | $113,971 | $125,280 |

These ratios exclude the shared $20m evaluation budget and employer and worker costs. B retention at 18 months and C employment at 12 months are different outcomes, so the ratios cannot rank the schemes directly. They also omit earnings gains for participants who would have remained employed anyway. Benefits need to be valued over time, against all relevant costs, before expansion. A comparison-group estimate can still be biased if participants have unobserved advantages.

The 95% real-earnings benchmark allows a limited transition loss while testing whether employment recovery masks a persistent pay cut. It is a proposed policy tolerance, not a research prediction. JSA should report earnings relative to matched workers as well as participants' own pre-program earnings, and include non-employed participants rather than measure only successful returners.

The 65% female and 20% age-50-plus participation benchmarks are provisional access safeguards. The female target is below women's 72% share of clerical employment, so meeting it alone would not establish fair access. The age target reserves attention for older workers but is not an estimate of their share of eligible applicants. DEWR should revise both using eligible-population data and compare application and acceptance rates across groups.

A costs more overall partly because it serves 100,000 people over four years, compared with B's 35,000 and C's 32,000. A's public cost is $4,320 per user including administration. Its lower unit cost does not prove greater effectiveness, just as its higher total cost does not prove inefficiency. The recommendation rests on the barriers each design addresses, with effectiveness still to be tested.

## Sensitivity and fiscal limits

- Increasing B participation by 10% adds approximately **$20m** across the programme.
- Doubling B participation, keeping its half-year launch profile, raises total package costs to approximately **$619m**.
- Adding 1,000 C participants each year adds approximately **$25m** over four years.
- A 10% increase in B's average lost net wage adds approximately **$20m**.
- A 10% increase in both schemes' participant unit costs, with unchanged administration rates and evaluation funding, adds approximately **$40m**.

I have estimated public spending here, rather than a full cost-benefit analysis. Employers also pay fees, and workers give up time and some earnings. These costs may affect whether they take part. Programme benefits must be estimated against a credible comparison, not inferred from economy-wide AI productivity projections.

Sources: ABS (2025), Bundesagentur für Arbeit (2024), DEWR (2024) and SkillsFuture Singapore (2024), linked in the [proposal reference list](proposal.md#references).
